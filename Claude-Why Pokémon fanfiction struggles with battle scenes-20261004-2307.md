Forum writers say battles are the hardest part of a Pokémon fic, and some admit they find writing them tedious and call them a necessary evil. That backs your logic: a writer who's bored by the scene produces a scene that reads bored, so your guess holds.

I only half agree with the sources, though, because their diagnosis stops at surface craft: describe body motion, come up with something readers haven't seen, look up movesets. I think what the games do well is make a battle a decision problem under constraints: six slots, four moves, a type chart, and no knowledge of what the opponent will do. The anime loosens those rules but covers it with animation, sound, and timing. Fic has neither, so writers either transcribe moves turn by turn or freestyle with no constraints, and then nothing can actually be lost. The nearest anyone gets to saying this is a post noting that attacks never seem to kill, and injuries should carry into the rest of the battle. What's missing is cost and legible rules, and battles are where that absence shows most. Another poster adds that a battle should advance plot or characterization, and when it doesn't, it's usually dire, which is the same point from the story side.

Mostly because the games are the only shared reference readers and writers have, so "follow the game rules" works as a safety net. One poster in the sources says outright that they try to keep battles logical to the games. The cost is that those rules are enforced by a system in the games and by nothing in prose, so copying them gives you the bookkeeping (type matchups, move lists, who has what) without the tension the system produced. You end up transcribing a turn-based game instead of writing a fight. That's why it reads flat.

So I agree: aim for the anime's energy. The anime itself discards the game rules whenever momentum or spectacle needs it, and the fights still work because the world holds to its own internal logic, not the games'. The only rules a writer has to honor are the ones they've set up in the story. Pacing is the same kind of choice. The anime doesn't have one tempo either: a reckless trainer's battle can be frantic and improvised, while a cold, calculating one can be slow and controlled, and the character's temperament should set that. A single fast pace for every fight would flatten them as much as a game-rules checklist does.

> Weighing how narrowly writers should source Pokémon lore.
>
> - Weighing how narrowly writers should source Pokémon lore.

It's a ceiling more than a disaster. Games-only gives a writer the rules layer and a shared vocabulary, but nothing about how to make a fight read as tense, fast, or costly, and that craft lives elsewhere. Pokémon doesn't even have a single canon to be faithful to: the anime, the manga, and the games already disagree with each other, so "follow the games" is picking one adaptation and treating it as the original.

Writers should absolutely pull from outside Pokémon. Hunter x Hunter is a strong example, since its Nen system is all rules and limits, yet fights turn on clever, specific uses of them and carry real cost. Sports manga like Haikyuu!! are another, because strategy and raw momentum live in the same scene, which is close to what a trainer battle wants. The web serials I mentioned earlier do the same thing in prose. So yes, a black-and-white rule is dumb as a rule. The part worth keeping is that whatever rules a story sets up, it has to honor them, and that doesn't depend on which source they came from.
